# GROUP: PHILOSOPHY (TipKind.PHILOSOPHY) — reflection and perspective.

# Mostly QUOTATIONS, each carrying the public-domain text it comes from so the attribution is
# checkable rather than taken on trust. A handful of ORIGINAL reflections at the end carry no
# source and claim none.
#
# WRITING RULE FOR THIS POOL: only quote what can be pinned to a specific chapter, letter, or
# section in a public-domain edition, and quote that edition's actual wording. Popular
# philosophy quotes are misattributed constantly: the thousand-miles line gets pinned on
# Confucius when it is Lao Tzu, "we are what we repeatedly do" is Will Durant summarising
# Aristotle rather than Aristotle, and "he who has a why to live can bear almost any how" is
# Viktor Frankl's paraphrase, not Nietzsche's sentence. Nietzsche's actual line is the one
# below. Two more worth knowing, because they read as if they belong here and do not: "the
# greatest weapon against stress is our ability to choose one thought over another" is not
# William James, and "be yourself in a world constantly trying to make you something else" is
# not Emerson. Every line below was checked against the linked edition's text, not against a
# quotations site.
#
# QUOTE THE EDITION, INCLUDING ITS SPELLING. The Hume line below reads "A wise Man, therefore,
# proportions his Belief to the Evidence" because that is how the 1748 edition sets it. The
# capitals are the eighteenth century, not a mistake, and modernising them would be an edit to
# a quotation made purely so it looked tidier on a phone. Leave them.
#
# SECOND WRITING RULE, on variety: this pool was once entirely Stoic plus a short tail (four
# Marcus, three Epictetus, three Seneca), which is a school, not a subject. Stoicism keeps the
# largest share because it suits the format, but the pool now also carries Confucian, Buddhist,
# Taoist, presocratic, rationalist, empiricist, German, transcendentalist, essayist and
# nineteenth-century voices, and the ratio is worth defending: before adding another Roman,
# check whether a tradition below has only one line in it.
#
# THIRD WRITING RULE, on length: a quotation has to survive a small widget, so prefer the
# sentence that is already short in the source over a long one trimmed with an ellipsis.
# Cutting at a comma, a colon or a semicolon the original already has (as with the Dhammapada
# and Lao Tzu lines below) is fine; cutting mid-clause and hoping nobody checks is how a
# quotation becomes a paraphrase.
#
# THE 2026-07-30 PASS, against the criticism recorded 2026-07-28. That criticism said the pool
# was conventional: every rule above is about being *accurate*, the pool clears that bar, none
# of them is about being worth stopping on, and it did not clear that one. What was here was
# the philosophy everyone has already met, in the register of a poster. The suspected cause was
# selecting for quotability, since a line short enough for a widget and instantly legible is
# usually a line that has been quoted to death.
#
# Seven lines were cut on exactly that test: the unexamined life, the journey of a thousand
# miles, to be great is to be misunderstood, optimism is the faith that leads to achievement,
# very little is needed to make a happy life, all that we are is the result of what we have
# thought, and I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately. Each was replaced
# from the *same tradition* rather than dropped, so the ratio the second rule defends came
# through unchanged: a different Lao Tzu chapter, a different Dhammapada verse, a different
# Heraclitus fragment, two more Thoreau and another Emerson.
#
# WHAT WAS SELECTED FOR INSTEAD, since "less famous" is not a standard. Two things. A line
# should need a beat to land, and it should cut against the reader rather than reassure them.
# "Even if you break your heart, none the less they will do just the same" is the clearest case
# of both: it takes a moment to parse, and what it says when it lands is that your distress is
# not an argument anyone else is obliged to hear. "For nonconformity the world whips you with
# its displeasure" is Emerson naming the cost rather than the reward. "But lo! men have become
# the tools of their tools" is a sentence a reader holding a phone is inside of.
#
# THE 2026-07-31 PASS PAID THE DEBT THE LAST ONE LEFT OPEN. That debt, in the previous header's
# own words: German and Enlightenment rationalist carried one line each, the pass before had
# replaced within traditions rather than widening the pool, and it had spent three of its five
# net additions on Stoics — the exact thing the second rule says to check for. The reason given
# was the *cost* of sourcing rather than what the pool needed, because Seneca and the
# Enchiridion are indexed section by section and the thin traditions are not. So this pass paid
# that cost and added no Romans at all: two Schopenhauer (German goes one to three), a second
# Spinoza on mirth (rationalist goes one to two), and Hume, who is a tradition this pool did
# not have at all. Four original reflections were added at the same time, which keeps the
# attributed share at 39 of 55 and well clear of the test that pins it.
#
# What that leaves open for the next pass: the pool is still entirely European and Chinese
# apart from the Dhammapada, and it has one woman in it. Wollstonecraft was read for this pass
# and left out on the third rule rather than on the second — the line worth having, "I do not
# wish them to have power over men; but over themselves", opens on a pronoun whose antecedent
# is two paragraphs back, and a widget has no room to supply it. That is a sourcing problem
# with a solution, not a reason the pool is shaped this way.
#
# Format is inline: "Tip text" alone, or "Tip text<TAB>Label<TAB>URL" for a quotation.
