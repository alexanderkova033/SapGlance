# GROUP: PHILOSOPHY (TipKind.PHILOSOPHY) — reflection and perspective.
#
# Mostly QUOTATIONS, each carrying the public-domain text it comes from so the attribution is
# checkable rather than taken on trust. A handful of ORIGINAL reflections at the end carry no
# source and claim none.
#
# WRITING RULE FOR THIS POOL: only quote what can be pinned to a specific chapter, letter, or
# section in a public-domain edition, and quote that edition's actual wording. Popular
# philosophy quotes are misattributed constantly: the thousand-miles line gets pinned on
# Confucius when it is Lao Tzu, "we are what we repeatedly do" is Will Durant summarising
# Aristotle rather than Aristotle, and "he who has a why to live can bear almost any how" is
# Viktor Frankl's paraphrase, not Nietzsche's sentence. Nietzsche's actual line is the one
# below. Two more worth knowing, because they read as if they belong here and do not: "the
# greatest weapon against stress is our ability to choose one thought over another" is not
# William James, and "be yourself in a world constantly trying to make you something else" is
# not Emerson. Every line below was checked against the linked edition's text, not against a
# quotations site.
#
# QUOTE THE EDITION, INCLUDING ITS SPELLING. The Hume line below reads "A wise Man, therefore,
# proportions his Belief to the Evidence" because that is how the 1748 edition sets it. The
# capitals are the eighteenth century, not a mistake, and modernising them would be an edit to
# a quotation made purely so it looked tidier on a phone. Leave them.
#
# SECOND WRITING RULE, on variety: this pool was once entirely Stoic plus a short tail (four
# Marcus, three Epictetus, three Seneca), which is a school, not a subject. Stoicism keeps the
# largest share because it suits the format, but the pool now also carries Confucian, Buddhist,
# Taoist, presocratic, rationalist, empiricist, German, transcendentalist, essayist,
# nineteenth-century, Persian, Indian, ancient Egyptian, Akan, Native American and
# twentieth-century voices, and the ratio is worth defending: before adding another Roman, check
# whether a tradition below has only one line in it. Several do.
#
# THIRD WRITING RULE, on length: a quotation has to survive a small widget, so prefer the
# sentence that is already short in the source over a long one trimmed with an ellipsis.
# Cutting at a comma, a colon or a semicolon the original already has (as with the Dhammapada
# and Lao Tzu lines below) is fine; cutting mid-clause and hoping nobody checks is how a
# quotation becomes a paraphrase.
#
# THE 2026-07-30 PASS, against the criticism recorded 2026-07-28. That criticism said the pool
# was conventional: every rule above is about being *accurate*, the pool clears that bar, none
# of them is about being worth stopping on, and it did not clear that one. What was here was
# the philosophy everyone has already met, in the register of a poster. The suspected cause was
# selecting for quotability, since a line short enough for a widget and instantly legible is
# usually a line that has been quoted to death.
#
# Seven lines were cut on exactly that test: the unexamined life, the journey of a thousand
# miles, to be great is to be misunderstood, optimism is the faith that leads to achievement,
# very little is needed to make a happy life, all that we are is the result of what we have
# thought, and I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately. Each was replaced
# from the *same tradition* rather than dropped, so the ratio the second rule defends came
# through unchanged: a different Lao Tzu chapter, a different Dhammapada verse, a different
# Heraclitus fragment, two more Thoreau and another Emerson.
#
# WHAT WAS SELECTED FOR INSTEAD, since "less famous" is not a standard. Two things. A line
# should need a beat to land, and it should cut against the reader rather than reassure them.
# "Even if you break your heart, none the less they will do just the same" is the clearest case
# of both: it takes a moment to parse, and what it says when it lands is that your distress is
# not an argument anyone else is obliged to hear. "For nonconformity the world whips you with
# its displeasure" is Emerson naming the cost rather than the reward. "But lo! men have become
# the tools of their tools" is a sentence a reader holding a phone is inside of.
#
# THE 2026-07-31 PASS PAID THE DEBT THE LAST ONE LEFT OPEN. That debt, in the previous header's
# own words: German and Enlightenment rationalist carried one line each, the pass before had
# replaced within traditions rather than widening the pool, and it had spent three of its five
# net additions on Stoics — the exact thing the second rule says to check for. The reason given
# was the *cost* of sourcing rather than what the pool needed, because Seneca and the
# Enchiridion are indexed section by section and the thin traditions are not. So this pass paid
# that cost and added no Romans at all: two Schopenhauer (German goes one to three), a second
# Spinoza on mirth (rationalist goes one to two), and Hume, who is a tradition this pool did
# not have at all. Four original reflections were added at the same time, which keeps the
# attributed share at 39 of 55 and well clear of the test that pins it.
#
# THE 2026-07-31 WIDENING, which is the debt above being paid rather than deferred again. Four
# voices, chosen so that three of them are traditions this pool did not have at all:
#
#   Zhuangzi (Taoist)   Joins Lao Tzu, taking the section from two to three. The obvious gap:
#                       Taoism was one book.
#   Saadi (Persian)     New. The Gulistan is a wisdom collection built out of exactly the length
#                       of line this format wants, which makes it the cheapest tradition here to
#                       source well.
#   Tagore (Indian)     New, and the only line in the pool with no translator between the reader
#                       and the author: Tagore wrote the English Gitanjali himself. Worth knowing
#                       because every other quotation here is somebody's rendering.
#   Anna Julia Cooper   New voice, and the second woman. Nineteenth-century American, alongside
#                       Douglass, which is the register she was writing in.
#
# TWO CHOICES INSIDE THAT WORTH RECORDING, because both were close.
#
# Cooper's famous sentence is the "when and where I enter" passage, and it is two hundred
# characters — unusable here, and cutting it would gut it. What is quoted instead is a plainer
# line from the same essay. The one deliberately *not* taken was "A race cannot be purified from
# without": it is more distinctively hers, and out of context on a home screen it reads as being
# about something it is not. Douglass survives that test because "if there is no struggle there
# is no progress" generalises; that Cooper line does not.
#
# The Zhuangzi line is cut at a semicolon the original has, which the third rule allows. The full
# sentence ends "...; the true Sage ignores reputation", which is the best of the three clauses
# and pushed the line to 93 characters. Losing the best clause to keep the line legible is the
# trade this pool makes every time, and it is worth noticing that it keeps costing something.
#
# THE 2026-08-02 PASS TOOK THE THREE GAPS THE LAST HEADER NAMED, which were African, indigenous,
# and anything written in the last hundred years. All three are now closed, and the previous
# header's diagnosis was right: they were a public-domain problem rather than a values one, and
# two of them were solved by finding the right edition rather than by relaxing any rule here.
#
#   Ptahhotep (Egyptian)  Maxim 24, in Gunn's 1906 translation. Africa, and also the oldest
#                         writing in this file by about two thousand years. The archaic register
#                         ("unto thee") is the 1906 edition's and stays, under the same rule that
#                         keeps Hume's capitals.
#   Akan proverb          The first line here attributed to a *people* rather than a person, and
#                         the first sub-Saharan one. See the note below, which is a caveat and
#                         not a footnote.
#   Ohiyesa               Charles A. Eastman, Santee Dakota, writing in English in 1911 — so, like
#                         Tagore, no translator stands between the reader and the author. Two
#                         lines rather than one, because a tradition that was wholly absent is not
#                         represented by a token.
#   Bertrand Russell      1930, and the reason the "last hundred years" gap existed at all: The
#                         Conquest of Happiness only entered the US public domain this January and
#                         only reached Gutenberg in February 2026. The gap was a copyright
#                         boundary, and that boundary moves one year every year. Worth checking
#                         again in a few years rather than treating the twentieth century as shut.
#
# THE AKAN CITATION CARRIES A COST AND IT IS NOT HIDDEN. The standard public-domain collection is
# Rattray's, and its full 1916 subtitle is "the primitive ethics of a savage people". The
# attribution here uses the short title, which is ordinary citation practice, but a reader who
# taps "why this tip?" and follows the link lands on the full one. That was judged better than
# leaving Africa out of the pool for a third pass; anyone who disagrees is disagreeing with a
# decision that was made rather than one that was missed. Rattray's own note on proverb 3 reads
# it as being about children on their backs seeing the sky better than their elders do, and the
# line was taken because it works without that note rather than because of it.
#
# TWO THINGS FOUND, VERIFIED AND LEFT OUT, so nobody re-derives them. Russell's "a generation
# that cannot endure boredom will be a generation of little men" is real and it is good, but the
# sentence in the book begins "For all these reasons a generation..." — quoting it means
# capitalising a lowercase word and starting mid-sentence. The third rule permits trimming a
# quotation's *tail* at punctuation the original has; trimming its head is a different move and
# this pool has never made it. And "Fear of public opinion, like every other form of fear, is
# oppressive and stunts growth" verified cleanly but says what Emerson's nonconformity line
# already says, better.
#
# A METHOD NOTE WORTH KEEPING. Every line in this pass was extracted by a summariser first and
# then checked against the raw source text, and the check earned its keep immediately: the
# Eastman line circulates, and was first proposed here, as "It *is* our belief". The book says
# "It was our belief". One word, one tense, and it is the difference between a quotation and a
# paraphrase of a dead man's beliefs about his own people.
#
# THE 2026-08-03 PASS TOOK THE TWO GAPS THAT LEFT, and one book closed both of them. Virginia
# Woolf's A Room of One's Own is 1929, so it is a woman *and* the near side of 1926, and it only
# became quotable here on 1 January 2025 when it entered the US public domain. That is the second
# time in two passes that a gap turned out to be a copyright date rather than a judgement — see
# Russell above — and it is now a habit worth keeping: when this pool looks narrow, check what
# came out of copyright this January before concluding anything about the pool.
#
#   Woolf, 1929           "One cannot think well, love well, sleep well, if one has not dined
#                         well." Chosen over the famous money-and-a-room sentence on the rule
#                         this pool has used since 2026-07-30: prefer the line that has not been
#                         quoted to death. It also happens to be the only line in the pool that
#                         argues the body has a claim on the mind, which on a wellness widget is
#                         the house position stated by someone who was not selling anything.
#   Stanton, 1892         "Alike mid the greatest triumphs and darkest tragedies of life we walk
#                         alone." A fourth woman, and a second nineteenth-century American voice
#                         beside Douglass and Cooper. The archaic "mid" is the 1892 address's own
#                         and stays, under the same rule that keeps Hume's capitals.
#
# Stanton lands in the pool's oldest argument rather than beside it: Montaigne's backshop,
# Schopenhauer on being alone, Emerson on nonconformity and Thoreau on quiet desperation are all
# about solitude as something chosen. Stanton's is solitude nobody chose, argued by someone
# making the case that this is exactly why a person must be equipped to bear it. That is the
# same subject cutting the other way, which is worth more to the pool than another voice
# agreeing with it.
#
# WHAT IS STILL OPEN. Wollstonecraft, again, and for the same reason as before: the line worth
# having, "I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves", opens on a pronoun
# whose antecedent is two paragraphs back, and a widget has no room to supply it. That is a
# sourcing problem with a solution rather than a reason the pool is shaped this way. The last
# hundred years is now two books deep rather than one, which is better and is not yet a range:
# Woolf and Russell were writing in the same decade, in the same country, at each other's
# distance from the same literary establishment. The next real widening there is a voice from
# somewhere else entirely, and it will most likely arrive the same way both of these did, on a
# January.
#

You can be wrong for years and still turn around in an afternoon.
Certainty is cheap. Notice what it costs you to change your mind.
The story you'll tell about today is already shorter than today is.
You'll never have more time than you have now, only different reasons.
The opinion you hold most confidently came from someone you have forgotten.
You cannot explain most of your life to anyone who was not there.
You have been wrong before with exactly this much confidence.
The advice you give most often is the advice you most need.
You will believe something next year that you would argue against today.
Everyone you find easy to dismiss is somebody's whole world.
What you notice is a choice you stopped making consciously long ago.
You are allowed to change your mind without announcing it.
